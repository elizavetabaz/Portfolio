I Spent Months Coaching the One Skill That's Still Not Automated

Looking back at a mentoring program from before AI wrote half the first drafts in marketing, and why the hardest part of the job was never generating content.

AI Community · 7 min read
Key Takeaways
  • →Production stopped being the bottleneck in marketing. Judgment is now the scarce skill.
  • →Mentoring through TechWomen centered on funnel mechanics and a scenario game about deciding with incomplete information.
  • →AI output is fluent but not always correct. Someone still has to check tone, accuracy, and how it lands across languages.
  • →Trust, not speed, is what separates brands using AI well from brands losing credibility with it.

A few years ago, I spent months mentoring a group of women who wanted to break into marketing, through the TechWomen program. No AI tools in the workflow. No prompt libraries. Just weekly sessions, real questions, and a lot of "here's how you actually structure a campaign."

Looking back at that experience now, in 2026, with AI writing half the first drafts in this industry, I keep landing on the same thought. The hardest part of marketing was never generating content. It was judging whether the content, and the decision behind it, was any good.

What mentoring actually covered

The curriculum wasn't glamorous. One session was pure pipeline mechanics: how a name moves from a captured lead to a marketing-qualified lead to something sales can actually act on, and why "more leads" and "better leads" are two different problems with two different fixes. We mapped out where lists actually come from (buy one, rent one, compile one, build one) and argued about which lead magnets earn attention versus which ones just generate noise. None of that required AI. It required understanding what each stage of a funnel is for before you touch a tool that promises to fill it faster.

The session I think about the most, though, wasn't about marketing theory at all. It was a game. Each person picked a number and got a scenario: a developer worried about low app downloads, a new sales manager with no idea how to loop you in, a director with a vague idea and high expectations, an HR lead with a morale problem and zero budget, a colleague in tears over a lost invoice and a request to keep it quiet. Five questions each. Then you had to decide what you'd actually do, with information you knew was incomplete.

That's the part I didn't expect to matter so much later. Not the funnel diagram. The five-questions problem: figuring out what's actually being asked, what's missing, and when you have enough to act versus when you're just stalling.

Why that matters more now, not less

Marketing in 2026 has a content problem, and it's not a shortage. Teams can generate more copy, more emails, more landing pages than ever, faster than ever. The bottleneck moved. It used to be production. Now it's judgment: deciding what's good enough to ship, and what's missing context nobody bothered to check.

I see this daily in my own work. AI drafts a newsletter segment in seconds. It's fluent, it's fast, and it's often wrong in ways that are easy to miss if you're not paying attention. The wrong tone for the segment. A claim that isn't quite true. A sentence that reads fine in English but falls flat once you think about how it lands in Romanian or Russian. None of that gets caught by the tool. It gets caught by someone running the same exercise as that mentoring game, just with a different scenario: here's an output, here's incomplete context, decide what you'd actually do with it.

The trend nobody's naming directly

A lot of "AI in marketing" content in 2026 is still framed as a speed story. How fast can you produce, how much can you automate, how many channels can one person cover. That's real, but it's not the interesting part anymore.

The interesting part is trust. Audiences are getting better at spotting generic AI output, and brands that ship it without review are losing credibility faster than they're gaining reach. The teams doing this well aren't the ones using AI the most. They're the ones who kept a human accountable for the last step, someone who reads the output and asks whether it actually sounds like the brand, and whether it's actually true.

That accountability isn't a technical skill. It's the same one from that mentoring group, back when there was no AI output yet to review, just a room of people learning to ask better questions before acting on incomplete information.

What stuck with me

I don't think any of us, myself included, set out to prepare for an AI-driven industry. We were just trying to get people hired and confident, and comfortable making calls without a full picture. But the fundamentals underneath that, knowing your audience, protecting a brand's voice, deciding when you actually have enough information to act, turned out to be exactly what didn't get replaced.

Tools change every year now. That part underneath them doesn't.
