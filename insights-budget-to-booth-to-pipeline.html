Budget to Booth to Pipeline: One Event, Start to Finish

How owning a sponsorship end to end, from contract negotiation to post-event reporting, kept one story from breaking across five different handoffs.

Events Demand Generation · 6 min read
Key Takeaways
  • →Owning the sponsorship budget from the negotiation table shapes what the entire campaign can do downstream.
  • →Warm and cold audiences need separate nurture logic, even for the same event.
  • →Post-event leads should be segmented by warmth, not dropped into one generic follow-up.
  • →A single owner across the whole funnel means one accountable report, not five disconnected ones.

Most B2B events get split across a lot of hands. Sales negotiates the sponsorship. Marketing runs the ads. Someone else builds the email flows. A different person mans the booth. By the time the leads land in a spreadsheet, nobody can say which part of the funnel actually worked.

Last spring I ran an event the other way. Endava sponsored Container Terminal Automation Conference Europe (CTAC2025) in Valencia, and I owned it end to end. Not a piece of it. All of it.

Before the contract was even signed

The work started at the negotiation table. I worked directly with the organisers on the sponsorship terms, signed the contract, and managed the budget for the whole thing. That part rarely gets mentioned in a marketing recap, but it shapes everything downstream. If you don't control the budget, you don't control what the campaign around the event can actually do.

Building demand before anyone landed in Valencia

Once the sponsorship was locked in, I built a pre-event awareness campaign on LinkedIn Ads, targeted at the audience most likely to walk past our booth: supply chain and logistics decision-makers across DACH and North America.

Alongside the ads, I built two separate nurture workflows in HubSpot. One went to our existing database, reminding warm contacts we'd be at the event. The other targeted companies confirmed to attend CTAC2025, a colder list that needed a different message and a different pace. Same event, two audiences, two logics. Treating them the same would have wasted both.

At the booth

I flew out for the event itself. I set up the booth, prepared the materials, and gave the company presentation on site. This is the part of demand generation that doesn't show up in a dashboard: standing in front of someone, explaining what we do, and deciding on the spot whether they're worth a follow-up call or a slower nurture track.

After everyone flew home

The leads didn't go cold once the booth came down. I built a post-event nurture workflow that picked up where the in-person conversation left off, segmented by how warm each contact actually was. Sales-ready contacts went straight to the sales team. Everyone else moved into a longer sequence instead of getting dropped.

Then came the reporting: leads by source, campaign performance, and how the sponsorship budget stacked up against what it generated. One report, one person accountable for every number in it.

Why this matters more than the event itself

The value here isn't that one person did every task alone. It's that nothing had to be handed off between strategy and execution. Other people helped write copy and design assets along the way, but I owned the thread that connected all of it: the budget, the targeting logic, the nurture sequencing, the booth, and the numbers at the end. When something didn't land, there was one person accountable for figuring out why, not a chain of departments pointing at each other.

That's what a full stack marketer actually does. Not touch every channel a little. Own the thread that runs through all of them, so the story doesn't break at the handoff.

If your events run through five different owners and nobody can tell you which part drove the pipeline, that's usually not a tooling problem. It's an ownership one.
